# AV Blocks

**Atrioventricular (AV) blocks** occur when conduction between the atria and ventricles is delayed or interrupted. They range from benign (1st degree) to immediately life-threatening (3rd degree).

## First-Degree AV Block

**PR interval > 200 ms**, constant, no dropped beats.

The impulse always gets through — just slowly. Often benign.
- Causes: vagal tone, inferior MI, digoxin, myocarditis, Lyme disease
- Treatment: none required (unless symptomatic or causing hemodynamic compromise)

## Second-Degree AV Block: Mobitz I (Wenckebach)

**PR interval progressively lengthens** until a QRS is dropped (the P wave with no following QRS), then the cycle resets.

Classic features:
- Grouped beating (the "footprints" of Wenckebach)
- The pause containing the dropped beat is less than twice the normal R–R
- The PR after the pause is shortest

**Site of block**: Usually within the AV node itself (proximal).
**Causes**: Inferior MI (RCA → AV node), increased vagal tone, myocarditis
**Prognosis**: Generally benign; often reversible

## Second-Degree AV Block: Mobitz II

**Fixed PR interval** with sudden dropped QRS complexes — without progressive PR prolongation.

Classic features:
- PR is constant (may be normal or prolonged)
- QRS drops without warning
- Often wide QRS (block is below the AV node, in the His-Purkinje system)

**Site of block**: Below the AV node (His or bundle branches). This is more dangerous.
**Causes**: Anterior MI, fibrosis, calcification of conduction system
**Prognosis**: Can progress to complete heart block; often requires pacemaker

## Distinguishing Mobitz I from Mobitz II

| Feature | Mobitz I | Mobitz II |
| --- | --- | --- |
| PR | Progressive lengthening | Fixed |
| Block site | AV node | Below AV node |
| QRS width | Usually narrow | Usually wide |
| Progression | Rarely progresses | Can progress to CHB |
| Causes | Inferior MI, vagal | Anterior MI, fibrosis |

## Third-Degree (Complete) AV Block

**No conduction** from atria to ventricles. P waves and QRS complexes fire completely independently — **AV dissociation**.

EKG features:
- P waves march through at sinus rate (60–100 bpm)
- QRS complexes march through at escape rate (< 60 bpm)
- **No relationship** between P and QRS
- QRS: narrow (junctional escape, 40–60 bpm) or wide (ventricular escape, 20–40 bpm)

Wide escape QRS = lower escape focus = worse prognosis.

**Treatment**: Immediate transcutaneous pacing; transvenous pacemaker placement.
