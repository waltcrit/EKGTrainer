# Atrial Fibrillation

**Atrial fibrillation (AFib)** is the most common sustained cardiac arrhythmia. It occurs when the atria fire in a chaotic, disorganized fashion at 350–600 impulses per minute — too fast and too irregular for coordinated atrial contraction.

## EKG features

All four of these must be present:

1. **Irregularly irregular R–R intervals** — the most important feature
2. **No distinct P waves** — replaced by chaotic "fibrillatory baseline" (f-waves)
3. **Narrow QRS** (usually) — unless aberrant conduction or pre-existing BBB
4. **Variable ventricular rate** — the AV node conducts randomly, typically 100–180 bpm (uncontrolled)

## How to recognize it

March out the R–R intervals: they will be completely random with no repeating pattern. Then look at the baseline between QRS complexes — instead of discrete P waves, you'll see low-amplitude, irregular undulations.

## Types of AFib

| Type | Duration |
| --- | --- |
| Paroxysmal | Terminates spontaneously within 7 days |
| Persistent | Lasts > 7 days; requires cardioversion |
| Long-standing persistent | > 12 months |
| Permanent | Accepted as permanent; no plan to restore sinus |

## Clinical significance

The two major risks of AFib:

1. **Stroke / thromboembolism**: Loss of coordinated atrial contraction → blood stasis in the left atrial appendage → thrombus → embolism. Risk assessed by **CHA₂DS₂-VASc score**.

2. **Tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy**: Sustained rapid ventricular response (> 100 bpm) impairs LV function over time.

## AFib with aberrancy vs Afib with pre-excitation

- **AFib + aberrancy**: irregularly irregular, wide QRS — looks like VT but rhythm is irregular
- **AFib + WPW (pre-excitation)**: irregularly irregular, very fast (> 200 bpm), extremely wide and bizarre QRS — **do NOT give AV nodal blockers** (adenosine, diltiazem, metoprolol, digoxin); they can cause VF
