# Sinus Tachycardia

**Sinus tachycardia** is sinus rhythm with a rate > 100 bpm. The SA node fires faster than normal, driven by physiologic demands or pathologic stimuli.

## Diagnostic criteria

- Rate: **> 100 bpm** (usually 100–160 bpm; can go higher with exertion)
- P waves: upright in II, present before every QRS
- PR: may shorten slightly at faster rates, but still 120–200 ms
- QRS: narrow (≤ 120 ms)
- Rhythm: regular

## Key principle: sinus tachycardia is a response, not a primary arrhythmia

Almost never do you treat sinus tachycardia directly. You find and treat the **cause**.

## Common causes

| Category | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| **Physiologic** | Exercise, anxiety, pain, fever |
| **Hypovolemia** | Dehydration, hemorrhage |
| **Infection** | Sepsis — tachycardia is an early sign |
| **Cardiac** | Heart failure (compensatory), pericarditis, myocarditis |
| **Pulmonary** | PE, pneumonia, hypoxia |
| **Metabolic** | Anemia, hyperthyroidism, hypoglycemia |
| **Drugs** | Epinephrine, albuterol, atropine, cocaine, amphetamines, caffeine |
| **Withdrawal** | Alcohol, benzodiazepines |

## Sinus tachycardia vs SVT

At rates above 150 bpm, sinus tachycardia and SVT can look similar. Key differentiating features:
- **Sinus tachy**: rate usually < 150; P waves clearly visible; gradual onset/offset; rate varies with breathing or vagal maneuvers (slows gradually)
- **SVT**: rate typically 150–250; abrupt onset/offset; P waves often buried in QRS or T wave; vagal maneuvers may terminate it suddenly (not just slow it)
