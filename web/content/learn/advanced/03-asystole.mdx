# Asystole

**Asystole** is the complete absence of electrical cardiac activity. There are no P waves, no QRS complexes, and no T waves — just a flat or nearly flat baseline.

## EKG features

- **Completely flat baseline** (isoelectric line)
- **No recognizable electrical activity** of any kind
- May have rare isolated P waves (P-wave asystole) — atrial activity still present, no ventricular

## Before calling asystole

Asystole is the most confirmed-but-wrong diagnosis in cardiac arrest. Before accepting it:

1. **Increase gain** (amplitude) on the monitor
2. **Check all leads** — fine VF may look like asystole in one lead
3. **Check connections** — a loose or disconnected lead gives a flat line
4. **Confirm in two leads** — true asystole should look flat in all leads

**Why it matters**: VF is treated with defibrillation; asystole is not. Defibrillating asystole is harmful. Distinguishing them is critical.

## Asystole vs fine VF

| Feature | Fine VF | Asystole |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Baseline | Irregular, undulating | Flat |
| Waveforms | Tiny irregular deflections | None |
| Response to defib | May convert to rhythm | No benefit |
| Gain | Turn up → more clear | Still flat |

## Treatment

Asystole has the worst prognosis of all cardiac arrest rhythms.

ACLS algorithm:
1. **CPR** immediately
2. **Epinephrine** 1 mg IV/IO every 3–5 minutes
3. **Treat reversible causes** (H's and T's):
   - H's: Hypovolemia, Hypoxia, Hydrogen ion (acidosis), Hypo/hyperkalemia, Hypothermia
   - T's: Tension pneumothorax, Tamponade, Toxins, Thrombosis (PE or MI)
4. **Do NOT defibrillate** asystole

## P-wave asystole

Occasionally, isolated P waves persist without QRS complexes. This indicates complete failure of ventricular conduction below the P wave. Management is the same as asystole — however, some of these patients respond to transcutaneous pacing if initiated early.
