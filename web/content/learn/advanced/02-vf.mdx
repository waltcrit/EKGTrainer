# Ventricular Fibrillation (VF)

**Ventricular fibrillation (VF)** is the most immediately lethal cardiac arrhythmia. The ventricles fire in a completely chaotic, disorganized pattern — no coordinated contraction, no cardiac output, no pulse.

## EKG features

- **No recognizable QRS complexes, P waves, or T waves**
- **Chaotic, irregular deflections** of varying amplitude and frequency
- **No organized waveforms** of any kind
- The baseline is completely disorganized

## Coarse vs fine VF

| Type | Amplitude | Clinical significance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Coarse VF** | High amplitude (> 3–5 mm) | Earlier VF; more likely to respond to defibrillation |
| **Fine VF** | Low amplitude (< 3 mm) | Longer duration VF; may resemble asystole |

Do not mistake fine VF for asystole — the management differs. If unsure, check another lead.

## VF vs artifact

Artifact can mimic VF. Before calling "code blue":
1. Check the patient — are they responsive? Do they have a pulse?
2. Check electrode connections
3. Look at a different lead
4. Artifact will have some underlying normal rhythm visible

## Causes of VF

- **Ischemia / acute MI** (most common)
- **Pre-excited AF in WPW** — rapid antegrade conduction via accessory pathway degenerates to VF
- **Long QT syndrome** (torsades degenerated to VF)
- **Brugada syndrome**
- **Hypokalemia/hypomagnesemia**
- **Electrocution**
- **Commotio cordis** (blunt chest trauma at specific cardiac cycle timing)

## Treatment

**Immediate defibrillation** — every second of delay reduces survival by ~10%

ACLS algorithm:
1. Confirm pulselessness → start CPR immediately
2. Defibrillate: 200 J biphasic (360 J monophasic)
3. Resume CPR 2 minutes
4. Epinephrine 1 mg IV/IO every 3–5 min
5. Defibrillate again
6. Amiodarone 300 mg IV (or lidocaine if unavailable)
7. Treat reversible causes (H's and T's)
