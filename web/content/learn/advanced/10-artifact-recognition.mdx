# Artifact Recognition

One of the most important skills in EKG reading is recognizing artifact — technical noise that mimics real cardiac abnormalities. Misidentifying artifact as pathology is a major source of unnecessary treatment and workups.

## What causes artifact?

- **Patient movement** (most common): tremor (Parkinson's, shivering), restlessness
- **Muscle artifact** (somatic tremor): high-frequency irregular noise superimposed on the trace
- **AC interference** (60 Hz in US, 50 Hz in Europe): regular, rapid, sinusoidal baseline noise
- **Lead connection problems**: disconnected, corroded, or poorly applied electrodes
- **Lead wire movement**: cables touching each other or vibrating

## Artifact patterns and what they mimic

| Artifact | What it mimics | Clue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Somatic tremor | AF, VF | Check other leads — often only in 1-2 leads; normal rhythm visible |
| Movement artifact | VT, VF | Sudden onset/offset; patient is moving; check pulse |
| AC interference | Flutter, sawtooth baseline | Regular 60 Hz (16.6 ms) frequency; perfectly regular |
| Loose electrode | ST elevation, block | Only in that lead's complex; bizarre without clinical correlation |
| Reversed limb leads | Axis deviation, new P morphology | Classic: Left arm-right arm reversal → lead I completely inverted (P, QRS, T all negative) |

## The key principle

**Artifact cannot be in only certain cardiac cycles** — real cardiac signals affect ALL cycles in that lead simultaneously. If you see changes in some beats but not others, and the pattern is inconsistent with the rhythm, suspect artifact.

## Arm lead reversal — the classic ECG error

Left arm / right arm (LA/RA) reversal is the most common lead placement error:

- Lead I: completely inverted (negative P, QRS, T)
- Lead II ↔ Lead III: swapped
- aVL ↔ aVR: swapped
- Precordial leads: unaffected

Clue: Negative P in lead I in a patient without dextrocardia is almost always LA/RA reversal.

## When in doubt

1. Repeat the EKG with fresh electrodes
2. Ensure the patient is warm, still, and not shivering
3. Use a limb that is away from medical devices
4. Ask: does this finding make sense given the clinical picture?

## Somatic tremor masquerading as atrial fibrillation

This is so common it has its own name. Parkinson's disease tremor (3–6 Hz) produces irregular baseline undulations that look exactly like AFib f-waves. Key clue: look for **regular P waves marching through** the noise — a P wave that appears at perfectly regular intervals means the underlying rhythm is sinus, not AFib.

The correct response: clean the electrodes, hold the limb still, repeat — not rate control for "AFib."
