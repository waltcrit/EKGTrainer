# ST Segment & J-Point

The **ST segment** is the short stretch between ventricular depolarization (end of QRS) and ventricular repolarization (T wave). The exact boundary between QRS and ST is the **J-point**. Almost every major rule for ischemia and STEMI asks what happens **right at that junction**.

---

## What the ST segment represents

The ST segment is the **early plateau** of ventricular repolarization — typically drawn close to the baseline when there is no acute injury current.

- **Elevation or depression** reflects a voltage shift relative to the **PR segment baseline** (usually TP when readable).
- **Morphology** (straight, upsloping, downsloping, concave vs convex) begins at the **J-point** and continues toward the T wave.

---

## The J-point — memorize this landmark

The **J-point** is where the **QRS meets the ST segment**. It is the anchor for:

| Teaching point | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| **STEMI criteria** | ST elevation is counted **at the J-point** in contiguous leads (with precordial vs limb thresholds per guideline). |
| **NSTEMI / ischemia** | Horizontal or downsloping depression is graded **from baseline to J-point**. |
| **Wide-QRS rules** | Modified Sgarbossa uses ST deviation **at the J-point** versus S-wave depth for discordance ratios. |
| **Early repolarization** | Benign pattern shows concave ST elevation **beginning at the J-point** (often with notching — “fishhook”). |

If you measure “somewhere in the middle” of the ST segment instead of at the J-point, you will disagree with textbooks, cath lab activation criteria, and serial comparisons.

---

## Beginner recognition checklist

1. Set baseline from the **PR segment** (or best isoelectric stretch before the QRS).
2. Find **last sharp inflection** at the end of QRS — that is your **J-point** even when takeoff is slurred.
3. Measure **vertical ST displacement at the J-point** in leads that matter for the clinical question.
4. Describe **shape after the J-point**: concave vs convex, upsloping vs horizontal vs downsloping into the T wave.
5. If QRS is wide (LBBB, paced), expect **discordant** ST-T shifts; use formal criteria (see Intermediate / Advanced lessons) — still measured **at the J-point**.

---

## Common pitfalls

- **Confusing tall T takeoff with STE:** Fix the J-point first; very symmetric ST–T fusion can mimic injury.
- **Ignoring slurred junction:** In IVCD/LBBB, hunt the J-point at the end of visible ventricular depolarization before declaring concordance vs discordance.
- **Mid-segment measurement:** Reserve non-J-point measures for research or explicit protocols — default clinical teaching is **J-point**.
