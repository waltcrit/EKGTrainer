# The T Wave

The **T wave** represents **ventricular repolarization** — the electrical reset of the ventricles after contraction. There is no separate wave for atrial repolarization; it is hidden within the QRS complex.

## Normal T wave

| Feature | Normal value |
| --- | --- |
| Direction | Same as QRS (concordant) in most leads |
| Amplitude | < 5 mm in limb leads; < 10 mm in precordial leads |
| Shape | Asymmetric: gradual upslope, steeper downslope |

In lead II: upright. In aVR: inverted. In V1: can be flat or inverted (normal).

## T wave abnormalities

| Finding | Think |
| --- | --- |
| **Peaked, tall, symmetric T waves** | Hyperkalemia, early STEMI ("hyperacute T") |
| **Inverted T waves** | Ischemia, LVH strain, RBBB, LBBB, cardiomyopathy |
| **Biphasic T waves** | Wellens syndrome (LAD ischemia) |
| **Flat T waves** | Hypokalemia, ischemia, non-specific |
| **Deeply inverted (Wellens A)** | Biphasic T in V2–V3 → critical LAD stenosis |
| **Deeply inverted (Wellens B)** | Deep symmetric inversion in V2–V3 → critical LAD stenosis |

## Concordance and discordance

In BBB and paced rhythms, T waves are expected to be **discordant** — opposite direction to the main QRS deflection. If they are **concordant** in this setting, it suggests superimposed ischemia (a "secondary" T wave change on top of a "primary" BBB change).

## Wellens syndrome

This is worth memorizing. Biphasic (Type A) or deeply inverted (Type B) T waves in V2–V3 in a patient with recent chest pain — even with a normal troponin — indicates critical proximal LAD stenosis. Do **not** stress test. This patient needs urgent angiography.

## Key teaching point

T waves are where ischemia hides. After assessing the ST segment, always look at T wave morphology, symmetry, and concordance.
