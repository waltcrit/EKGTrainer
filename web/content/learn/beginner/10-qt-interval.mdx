# The QT Interval

The **QT interval** is measured from the start of the QRS complex to the end of the T wave. It represents the total duration of ventricular depolarization and repolarization.

<DiagramPQRST highlight={["qrs", "st", "t", "qt"]} />

## Why it matters

A prolonged QT interval predisposes to a dangerous ventricular arrhythmia called **torsades de pointes** (TdP) — a polymorphic VT that can degenerate into ventricular fibrillation and sudden death.

This is why QT measurement is a required step in every systematic EKG read.

## Correcting for heart rate: QTc

The QT interval naturally shortens as heart rate increases. To compare QT across different heart rates, we use the **corrected QT (QTc)** using Bazett's formula:

**QTc = QT ÷ √(R–R in seconds)**

**Normal QTc:**
- Men: ≤ 440 ms
- Women: ≤ 460 ms
- Borderline prolonged: 440–500 ms
- High risk: > 500 ms

## Causes of prolonged QTc

**Drugs (most common):**
- Antiarrhythmics: amiodarone, sotalol, quinidine, dofetilide
- Antibiotics: azithromycin, moxifloxacin, erythromycin
- Antipsychotics: haloperidol, droperidol, quetiapine
- Antiemetics: ondansetron, metoclopramide
- Check every new medication

**Electrolytes:**
- Hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, hypocalcemia (the "3 H's")

**Cardiac:**
- Myocarditis, cardiomyopathy, post-cardiac arrest

**Congenital:**
- Long QT syndrome (LQTS types 1, 2, 3)

## Short QTc (< 340 ms)

Hypercalcemia, digitalis toxicity, short QT syndrome (rare, high risk of VF).
