# What Is an ECG?

An **electrocardiogram (ECG or EKG)** is a recording of the electrical activity of the heart over time. It does not measure blood flow, contractile force, or oxygen levels — only electrical signals.

## How it works

The heart generates electrical impulses that travel through cardiac tissue in a predictable sequence. Electrodes placed on the skin detect these signals from different angles (leads), and the machine plots voltage over time on a graph.

- **X-axis** → time (standard speed: 25 mm per second)
- **Y-axis** → voltage (standard calibration: 10 mm per millivolt)

Each "lead" is essentially a different camera angle on the same electrical event.

## The 12-lead ECG

A standard ECG has **12 leads**, grouped as:

| Group | Leads | View |
|---|---|---|
| Limb leads | I, II, III | Frontal plane |
| Augmented leads | aVR, aVL, aVF | Frontal plane |
| Precordial leads | V1–V6 | Horizontal plane |

A **rhythm strip** is a single lead (usually lead II or V1) recorded over a longer period — used to assess rhythm.

## What you're looking at

Each cardiac cycle produces a characteristic set of deflections:

- **P wave** — atrial depolarization (atria contract)
- **QRS complex** — ventricular depolarization (ventricles contract)
- **T wave** — ventricular repolarization (ventricles reset)

The next lesson covers the rhythm strip in detail.

## Key takeaway

An ECG records electrical activity — not mechanical activity. A patient can have a normal-looking ECG and still have a structural heart problem (e.g., heart failure). Conversely, an abnormal ECG doesn't always mean a sick patient. Context always matters.
