# A Systematic Approach to Reading an EKG

The most important habit you can build as an EKG reader is this: **read every EKG the same way, every time.**

Expert readers don't skip steps because a pattern is "obvious." They follow the same sequence on every strip — for good reason. The dangerous rhythms are often found in the step you would have skipped.

## The 17-step checklist

Use this sequence on every strip. Step numbering matches the canonical checklist in **`docs/analysis_steps.md`** in this repository — step through in order, even if a diagnosis seems clear after step 2.

## Why these steps, in this order?

**Steps 1–2** anchor validity — confirm **patient/context**, then **prior tracings** so “new” versus “old” change drives urgency.

**Steps 3–5** establish **rate**, **rhythm category**, and whether **each QRS is preceded by organized atrial activity** — the backbone for sinus versus AF/flutter versus AV dissociation thinking.

**Step 6** measures **intervals** (PR, QRS, QT) — early gate for block, pre-excitation, wide-QRS mimics, and pro-arrhythmic QT risk.

**Steps 7–12** walk morphology and repolarization in depth: **P-wave morphology**, **axis**, **precordial progression**, then **ST segments** (always **anchor displacement at the J-point**), **T waves**, and **Q waves**.

**Steps 13–17** integrate **named cross-lead syndromes** — ACS mimics (including wide-QRS rules), strain versus ischemia, pulmonary patterns, pericarditis versus benign early repolarization, then channelopathic / structural clues.

Early practice can emphasize **1–12** until automatic; **13–17** reward deliberate rehearsal once baseline morphology is solid.

## A word on pattern recognition

As you read more EKGs, you will start to recognize rhythms at a glance. This is good. But don't let pattern recognition replace the systematic approach — use it to generate a hypothesis at step 1, then confirm or deny it with each subsequent step.

The systematic approach is what catches the thing you weren't expecting.
