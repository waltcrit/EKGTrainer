# EKG Waveform Components: Definitions, Morphology, and Physiology

## BASELINE AND INTERVALS

### Baseline (Isoelectric Line)

**Definition:** The horizontal reference line representing zero electrical potential, used to measure segment and wave displacements.

**Physiology:** Appears when there is no electrical activity in the heart. The baseline is typically defined as the TP segment (period between end of T wave and onset of P wave) when the atria and ventricles are both at rest.

**Normal Characteristics:**

- Completely flat, without oscillation or drift
- Represents the resting state between electrical events
- Used as the zero reference for all other measurements

**Abnormalities:**

- **Wandering baseline:** Slow drift up/down, usually from poor electrode contact or patient movement
- **60 Hz interference:** Regular oscillation from electrical noise
- **Artifact:** Irregular deflections from muscle tremor, electrode movement, or electrocautery

## ATRIAL DEPOLARIZATION

### P Wave

**Definition:** The positive (or biphasic) deflection representing atrial depolarization, originating from the sinoatrial (SA) node.

**Physiology:** The SA node generates an electrical impulse that spreads through the right atrium, then crosses the Bachmann bundle to the left atrium. This coordinated depolarization produces the P wave. The wave progresses from the SA node area (high right atrium) toward the AV node.

**Normal Characteristics:**

- **Amplitude:** <2.5 mm (0.25 mV) in the limb leads; <3 mm in precordial leads
- **Duration:** <120 ms (0.12 s, 3 small squares)
- **Morphology:** Smooth, rounded deflection; usually biphasic in V1
- **Polarity in lead II:** Upright (positive) in normal sinus rhythm
- **Polarity in lead aVR:** Downward (negative) in normal sinus rhythm
- **Axis:** Between –30° and +90° (normal P axis)

**Normal Variations:**

- Biphasic in leads V1, aVL, III (normal finding)
- May be inverted in aVR, V1 (normal)
- Small notches or ripples may be present

**Abnormalities:**

- **Peaked (tall) P waves** (>2.5 mm in II, III, aVF): Right atrial enlargement (RAE), COPD, pulmonary hypertension
- **Broad, notched P waves** (≥120 ms): Left atrial enlargement (LAE), mitral valve disease, hypertension
- **Biphasic with deep negative component in V1:** LAE with right atrial involvement
- **Absent P waves:** Atrial fibrillation, junctional rhythm, ectopic atrial pacing
- **Inverted P waves in II:** Ectopic atrial rhythm, retrograde conduction (junctional origin)
- **Tall, peaked P in V1:** Chronic lung disease (P pulmonale)
- **Multiform P waves:** Wandering atrial pacemaker, atrial irritability

### PR Segment

**Definition:** The horizontal segment between the end of the P wave and the beginning of the QRS complex, representing the time when the impulse travels through the AV node and bundle of His.

**Physiology:** After the P wave ends, atrial depolarization is complete. The impulse is delayed as it passes through the AV node (physiologic delay to allow atrial contraction to complete before ventricular depolarization). The impulse then rapidly travels down the bundle of His and bundle branches. During this period, there is no electrical activity visible on the surface ECG.

**Normal Characteristics:**

- Flat, at baseline (isoelectric)
- Duration varies with PR interval
- Usually 30–50 ms duration (excluding the PR interval measurement)

**Abnormalities:**

- **PR depression:** Atrial infarction, pericarditis (Stage 1), ectopic atrial rhythm
- **Sagging appearance:** Atrial ischemia, pericarditis

## ATRIOVENTRICULAR CONDUCTION

### PR Interval

**Definition:** The time interval from the beginning of the P wave to the beginning of the QRS complex, measuring total AV conduction time (atrial depolarization + AV node delay + ventricular conduction initiation).

**Physiology:** Represents the complete journey of the electrical impulse from the SA node through atrial depolarization, delay in the AV node, and conduction down the bundle of His to the bundle branches. The bulk of the delay occurs in the AV node, providing a physiologic pause that allows atrial mechanical contraction to be completed before ventricular depolarization begins.

**Normal Characteristics:**

- **Duration:** 120–200 ms (0.12–0.20 s; 3–5 small squares)
- **Constant in sinus rhythm** (minimal beat-to-beat variation)
- Equal PR interval for all sinus beats in a regular rhythm

**Abnormalities:**

- **Prolonged PR interval** (>200 ms): 1st degree AV block, normal variant (athletes), medications (beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, digitalis), hyperkalemia, myocarditis
- **Short PR interval** (<120 ms): Wolff-Parkinson-White (WPW) syndrome, Lown-Ganong-Levine syndrome, junctional rhythm, atrial origins close to AV node
- **Variable PR intervals:** 2nd degree AV block (Mobitz I), irregular atrial rhythm
- **Progressive PR prolongation:** Wenckebach (Mobitz I), suggesting AV nodal fatigue

## VENTRICULAR DEPOLARIZATION

### QRS Complex

**Definition:** The three-part deflection representing ventricular depolarization: Q wave (initial negative), R wave (initial positive), and S wave (negative following R).

**Physiology:** Ventricular depolarization begins at the interventricular septum (left-to-right), followed by the left ventricle, then right ventricle. The electrical wave spreads radially outward from the bundle branches to the ventricular myocardium. The overall duration reflects the time needed for complete ventricular muscle depolarization and is heavily influenced by the bundle branch system.

**Normal Characteristics:**

- **Duration:** <120 ms (0.12 s; less than 3 small squares)
- **Axis:** Between –30° and +90° (normal QRS axis)
- **Shape:** Smooth, without notches (except normal septal notching in some leads)
- **Voltage:** Varies by lead; generally <25 mm in limb leads, <30 mm in precordial leads

**QRS Components:**

#### Q Wave

- **Definition:** First negative deflection of the QRS complex (before the first positive deflection)
- **Normal characteristics:**
  - Small (<40 ms duration; 1 small square) in most leads
  - Shallow (<25% of R wave height or <2.5 mm)
  - Septal Q waves are normal in leads I, aVL, V5–V6 (from normal septal depolarization)
  - Normal in inferior leads (II, III, aVF) when <40 ms and <25% of R wave
- **Abnormal Q waves (pathological):**
  - **Duration ≥40 ms** and **depth ≥25% of R wave height**
  - Indicates prior transmural myocardial infarction (scar tissue)
  - Specific to territory: anterior (V1–V4), inferior (II, III, aVF), lateral (I, aVL, V5–V6)
  - Pathological Q waves persist long-term after MI (old MI pattern)

#### R Wave

- **Definition:** First positive deflection of the QRS complex
- **Normal characteristics:**
  - Present in precordial leads; height increases from V1 to V5 (R wave progression)
  - V1 usually minimal or absent
  - V5–V6 typically tallest
  - May be small or absent in right lateral leads (normal)
- **Abnormalities:**
  - **Poor R wave progression:** Loss of normal increase in height from V1 to V5, seen in anterior MI, LBBB, WPW, LVH, obesity, normal variant in tall thin patients
  - **Tall R waves in V1–V2:** Posterior MI (mirror image), WPW with posterior pathway
  - **R' wave (double R peak):** RBBB pattern, WPW, RVOT tachycardia

#### S Wave

- **Definition:** Negative deflection following the R wave
- **Normal characteristics:**
  - May be absent (normal variant)
  - If present, usually small
  - Progressively decreases from V1 to V6
  - Deeper in V1–V2, shallower in V5–V6
  - Small S wave in lateral leads (I, V5–V6) is normal
- **Abnormalities:**
  - **Deep S waves in V1–V3 with LBBB:** Indicates left ventricular electrical forces
  - **Persistent S wave in V5–V6:** RBBB or RVH
  - **Deep S in lead I:** RBBB, right axis deviation, or posterior wall MI

### QRS Axis

**Definition:** The average direction of the net QRS electrical vector in the frontal plane, representing the overall direction of ventricular depolarization.

**Physiology:** The QRS axis reflects the normal left-to-right, superior-to-inferior spread of ventricular depolarization. Normal axis is 0° to +90° (downward and leftward), which corresponds to the anatomic orientation of left ventricular dominance.

**Normal Characteristics:**

- **Normal axis:** Between –30° and +90°
- **Left axis deviation (LAD):** Between –30° and –90° (abnormal)
- **Right axis deviation (RAD):** Between +90° and +180° (abnormal)
- **Extreme right axis:** Between –90° and ±180° (very abnormal, usually artifact or lead reversal)

**Abnormalities:**

- **Left axis deviation (–30° to –90°):** LAFB, inferior MI with lateral extension, LVH, obesity, older age, normal variant in tall thin patients
- **Right axis deviation (+90° to +180°):** RBBB, chronic lung disease (COPD), RAE, lateral MI, pregnancy, normal in tall thin patients
- **Extreme axis (±90° to ±180°):** Lead reversal, emphysema, dextrocardia, epicardial pacing, severe hyperkalemia

## VENTRICULAR REPOLARIZATION

### ST Segment

**Definition:** The flat segment connecting the end of the QRS complex (J point) to the beginning of the T wave, representing the period of early ventricular repolarization.

**Physiology:** After ventricular depolarization is complete, the ventricles are fully depolarized and before repolarization begins. During this period, the entire ventricular myocardium is in a similar electrical state (plateau phase of the action potential), resulting in zero net electrical activity and a flat baseline.

**Normal Characteristics:**

- **Position:** At baseline (isoelectric), ±0.5 mm displacement acceptable
- **Shape:** Completely flat
- **Duration:** Variable, inversely related to heart rate
- **Concave upward in precordial leads** (slightly curved upward): normal variant
- **Slight ST elevation** (<1 mm) in V2–V3 (especially V2) is normal, particularly in young males

**Normal Variants:**

- **Early repolarization:** Benign concave ST elevation (1–3 mm) in precordial and inferior leads, especially in young African-American males
- **Juvenile T wave pattern:** Persistent T wave inversions in V1–V2 in young patients (normal)

**Abnormalities:**

- **ST elevation ≥1 mm in two contiguous leads:** STEMI, pericarditis, early repolarization, Brugada syndrome, LVH with strain, hyperkalemia
- **ST depression ≥1 mm:** NSTEMI/ischemia, posterior MI (mirror), LVH with strain, pulmonary embolism, tachycardia, normal variant with rapid rate
- **Horizontal or downsloping ST depression:** More concerning for ischemia than upsloping
- **Prolonged ST elevation:** Aneurysm, pericarditis (chronic stage), scar tissue
- **Sagging ST segment:** Digitalis effect, hypokalemia

### J Point

**Definition:** The junction point where the QRS complex ends and the ST segment begins, marked anatomically by the junction of depolarization and early repolarization.

**Normal Characteristics:**

- Should be at or very near the baseline
- Reference point for measuring ST displacement
- Ideally isoelectric in normal ECG

**Abnormalities:**

- **J point elevation:** Brugada syndrome, early repolarization, hypothermia (Osborn wave), hyperkalemia
- **J point depression:** Myocardial ischemia, strain patterns

### T Wave

**Definition:** The larger, usually smooth deflection representing ventricular repolarization, the electrical recovery of the ventricular myocardium.

**Physiology:** Ventricular repolarization occurs more slowly and asynchronously than depolarization, beginning at the epicardium and proceeding toward the endocardium (opposite to depolarization direction). This creates electrical forces that are generally opposite to the QRS forces, producing a T wave that is usually in the same direction as the major QRS deflection in most leads.

**Normal Characteristics:**

- **Duration:** Approximately 100–160 ms (2.5–4 small squares)
- **Amplitude:** <5 mm in limb leads; <10 mm in precordial leads
- **Polarity in lead II:** Upright (positive) in normal sinus rhythm
- **Polarity in lead aVR:** Usually inverted (negative) — normal
- **Polarity in precordial leads:** Upright from V2/V3 through V6; may be inverted in V1
- **Shape:** Smooth, symmetric, rounded apex
- **T wave inversion in lead aVR and V1 is normal**

**Normal Variations:**

- **Asymmetric T waves:** Normal in precordial leads (slow upslope, rapid downslope)
- **Biphasic T waves:** Normal in V1, aVR, sometimes III (normal variant)
- **Shallow inversions in lateral leads:** Normal if <1 mm
- **Juvenile T wave pattern:** T wave inversions in V1–V2 in children and young adults (normal)

**Abnormalities:**

- **Inverted T waves in II, III, aVF:** Inferior wall ischemia/MI, inferior strain pattern
- **Inverted T waves in V1–V4:** Anterior wall ischemia/MI, anterior strain pattern, pulmonary embolism (V1–V4), LVH with strain
- **Deep, symmetric T wave inversions:** Wellens syndrome (LAD ischemia), MI, critical ischemia
- **Biphasic T waves in V2–V3:** Wellens Type A pattern (LAD reperfusion/critical ischemia)
- **Tall, peaked T waves:** Hyperkalemia, early MI (hyperacute phase), LVH
- **Flattened T waves:** Hypokalemia, ischemia, pulmonary embolism, pericarditis (Stage 2), normal variant
- **Prolonged, broad T waves:** Electrolyte abnormalities, MI evolution
- **T wave alternans:** Arrhythmogenic risk, pericardial effusion with swinging heart, critical ischemia
- **U wave fusion with T wave:** Hypokalemia, prolonged QT

### QT Interval

**Definition:** The time interval from the beginning of the QRS complex to the end of the T wave, measuring the total duration of ventricular depolarization and repolarization.

**Physiology:** Represents the entire electrical systole of the ventricles — the period during which the ventricles are either depolarizing or repolarizing. This interval is highly rate-dependent: faster heart rates shorten the QT interval, slower rates lengthen it.

**Normal Characteristics:**

- **Corrected QT (QTc) using Bazett formula:** QTc = QT / √(RR interval)
  - **QTc <450 ms in males**
  - **QTc <460 ms in females**
  - Some sources use <440 ms and <450 ms respectively
- **Uncorrected QT varies with heart rate:** Approximately 300–450 ms at rates of 60–100 bpm
- **QTc should be roughly equal across all beats in sinus rhythm**

**Measurement Technique:**

- Measure from the beginning of the Q wave to the end of the T wave (where T returns to baseline)
- If U wave present, ignore it and measure only to T wave end
- Use lead with longest, clearest QT interval (often V2, V3, or II)
- Measure multiple beats and average

**Abnormalities:**

- **Prolonged QTc ≥500 ms:** Long QT syndrome, medications (antiarrhythmics, antipsychotics, antibiotics), electrolyte abnormalities (hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, hypocalcemia), myocarditis, bradycardia
- **Shortened QTc:** Hypercalcemia, hyperkalemia (mild), digoxin toxicity
- **QT dispersion (variability >60 ms between leads):** Increased arrhythmia risk, ischemia, cardiomyopathy
- **QT prolongation with risk of Torsades de Pointes:** Long QT syndrome, acquired causes, electrolyte abnormalities

## LATE REPOLARIZATION AND RECOVERY

### U Wave

**Definition:** A small deflection sometimes visible after the T wave, representing the final phase of ventricular repolarization (repolarization of the Purkinje system/ventricular tissue).

**Physiology:** The U wave reflects the late, slow repolarization of specialized conduction tissue (Purkinje fibers) or the papillary muscles. It is mechanically absent in most individuals but becomes visible when there is prolonged repolarization or altered electrolyte states.

**Normal Characteristics:**

- **Very small:** Typically <0.5 mm amplitude, usually <25% of T wave amplitude
- **Polarity:** Same direction as the T wave (usually upright in II)
- **Duration:** Short, follows T wave with visible separation
- **Visibility:** May not be visible on every ECG in normal individuals
- **Best seen in:** Precordial leads (V2–V4), especially at slower heart rates

**Abnormalities:**

- **Tall, prominent U waves:** Hypokalemia (classic finding), bradycardia, LVH, athletes with slow rates
- **Inverted U waves:** Severe hypokalemia, ischemia, arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (ARVC)
- **U wave fusion with T wave:** Hypokalemia with prolonged repolarization, making the whole T-U complex appear as one large deflection
- **Widened U wave:** Electrolyte abnormalities, prolonged repolarization
- **Bifid U wave:** Hyperkalemia, ventricular ectopy

### TP Segment

**Definition:** The flat segment between the end of the T wave (or U wave if visible) and the beginning of the next P wave, representing complete electrical silence between atrial and ventricular depolarization.

**Physiology:** This is the true baseline of the ECG, when both atria and ventricles are completely polarized and at rest, with no electrical activity occurring.

**Normal Characteristics:**

- **Completely flat, at baseline level**
- **Used as the reference isoelectric line for measuring ST displacement**
- **Duration varies with heart rate and PR interval**

**Abnormalities:**

- **Elevated or depressed TP segment:** Artifact, baseline drift, or reflects PR segment changes (abnormal TP baseline suggests abnormal PR segment or ST segment)
